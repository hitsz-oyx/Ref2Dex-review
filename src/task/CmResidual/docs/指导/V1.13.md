DExplore does have a notion of phase, but it is **implicit rather than an explicit phase variable**. And this changes how I think we should proceed.

The main thing I would *not* do now is either (a) blindly keep the frozen DExplore checkpoint as our base, or (b) introduce a hard “pause the reference until grasp succeeds” state machine as the core solution.

## 1. Why DExplore does not need an explicit phase variable

Look at the original DExplore update:

$$
t \leftarrow t+1
$$

every physics/control step, and its observation is built using

$$
r_{t+1},\qquad r_{t+16}.
$$

The code literally uses `progress_buf` as the reference time and clamps `ts + delta_t`; the final 1442-D observation is the concatenation of the +1 and +16 observations.

So DExplore actually has:

$$
\boxed{\phi_t=t}
$$

It just never feeds a scalar called `phase` into the network.

The reference itself encodes the phase:

$$
(r_{t+1},r_{t+16})
$$

is usually enough for the policy to infer “I am approaching,” “I am closing,” “I am lifting,” etc.

So saying “DExplore has no phase” is slightly misleading.

It has a **fixed clock phase**.

---

## 2. More importantly: DExplore does not wait when it fails

This is where my previous phase-hold suggestion differed from DExplore.

DExplore does **not** say:

> “I failed to grasp, so stop the reference.”

Instead:

$$
t\rightarrow t+1
$$

continues.

The policy is trained so that it stays sufficiently close to the moving reference.

If it deviates too far, DExplore has early termination criteria based on hand/object/interaction deviation. The paper calls these **adaptive spatial scopes / time-varying termination boundaries**. ([Proceedings of Machine Learning Research][1])

The released code contains, for example, hand and object deviation termination tests as well as interaction/contact termination conditions.

So DExplore's philosophy is actually:

$$
\boxed{\text{keep up with the reference, or terminate and try again}}
$$

not:

$$
\boxed{\text{slow the reference down until the policy catches up}}.
$$

That is an important distinction.

---

# 3. How was DExplore actually trained?

The Inspire configuration is revealing.

It uses:

* **8192 environments**
* 30 Hz control (`controlFrequencyInv: 2`)
* `stateInit: Start`
* early termination enabled
* 1442-D observations
* 18-D actions.

Its teacher is PPO with a fairly large MLP:

$$
1442
\rightarrow1024
\rightarrow1024
\rightarrow1024
\rightarrow512
\rightarrow18.
$$

The official PPO configuration uses horizon 64, minibatch 16384 and up to 100,000 epochs.

And this is not trained on a single trajectory.

The released teacher was trained using **658 GRAB sequences**, together with Isaac Gym rollouts. ([GitHub][2])

Its reward combines substantially more than hand-pose imitation:

$$
r=
r_{\text{hand}}
\cdot
r_{\text{object}}
\cdot
r_{\text{interaction}}
\cdot
r_{\text{contact}}
\cdot
r_{\text{energy}}
$$

conceptually.

The source explicitly contains:

* fingertip/body tracking,
* object translation/orientation tracking,
* interaction-graph matching,
* contact matching,
* velocity/control penalties,
* early termination.

And the paper's central idea is deliberately *not* strict motion tracking. Human demonstrations are treated as **soft guidance inside a reference-dependent spatial envelope**. ([Proceedings of Machine Learning Research][1])

So DExplore learns:

$$
\pi_{\rm DX}(s_t,r_{t+1},r_{t+16})
$$

through millions/billions of simulated transitions until it learns how to remain synchronized with those types of reference motions.

---

# 4. This explains the DexYCB failure much better

This is the part I think we were misunderstanding.

DExplore did not solve a generic problem of:

$$
\text{arbitrary reference}
\rightarrow
\text{track it}.
$$

It solved something closer to

$$
\text{GRAB-derived reference distribution}
\rightarrow
\text{physically feasible Inspire behavior}.
$$

The released model card is particularly important here: the teacher is trained on 658 GRAB sequences, and its evaluation does **not** define a conventional held-out sequence/object split; evaluation reference objects remain from the GRAB object set. ([GitHub][2])

Therefore its good performance does not establish:

$$
\pi_{\rm DX}
(\text{arbitrary DexYCB reference})
\approx
\text{good tracking}.
$$

Our experiment is asking it to extrapolate to:

$$
\text{DexYCB human}
\rightarrow
\text{our Inspire retargeting}
\rightarrow
\text{synthetic DExplore-compatible reference}.
$$

That is a substantial OOD shift.

So I currently think the most probable explanation for the bad DexYCB behavior is **not missing phase**.

It is:

$$
\boxed{\text{frozen DExplore is far outside its learned reference distribution}}
$$

plus potentially some remaining reference-semantic mismatch.

---

# 5. Then should we copy DExplore and train another base?

If the only goal were maximum manipulation performance, the safest option probably would be:

$$
\text{DExplore checkpoint}
\rightarrow
\text{fine-tune on our retargeted DexYCB dataset}.
$$

Not train from scratch—fine-tune.

I expect that to work much better than frozen zero-shot transfer.

But **I don't think that is the best choice for our project.**

Why?

Because then DExplore would itself learn:

* contact correction,
* grasp adjustment,
* object manipulation,
* embodiment adaptation.

Those are exactly the phenomena we want OI-Cm + residual learning to explain.

We would end up with

$$
\text{powerful fine-tuned DExplore}
+
\text{small residual}.
$$

Then if manipulation succeeds, we cannot tell whether Cm contributed anything meaningful.

Scientifically, that weakens our method.

---

# 6. I think the most likely clean solution is different

I would use:

$$
\boxed{\text{retargeted trajectory as a deterministic nominal controller}}
$$

and train **only the residual** to remain synchronized with the fixed reference clock.

But—and this is the important modification—I would **not train residual PPO only with complete episodes starting at frame 0**.

That is the real solution to the credit-assignment problem you raised.

---

# 7. Keep the reference clock fixed

During the final task:

$$
\phi_{t+1}=\phi_t+1.
$$

No contact-based freezing.

Thus:

$$
q^{0}_t=q^{ref}_{t+1}
$$

and

$$
q^{target}_t
=
q^{ref}_{t+1}
+
\Delta q_t^{RL}.
$$

Why keep a fixed clock?

Because ultimately the task is to reproduce a dynamic interaction, including timing.

If we allow arbitrary phase stopping, the policy could learn:

> “I'm not sure what to do, so stay in grasp forever.”

That technically avoids failure but does not perform the demonstration.

A hard phase gate can therefore hide rather than solve the control problem.

---

# 8. But during training, don't force the policy to solve the whole sequence immediately

This is where I think we should borrow DExplore's idea, but not its entire policy.

Suppose the reference contains 100 frames:

```text
0 -------- 30 ------- 45 -------- 60 ---------- 100
 approach    contact     grasp        lift
```

Don't initially train only:

$$
0\rightarrow100.
$$

Instead sample a starting reference index:

$$
k\sim p(k)
$$

and initialize around the corresponding state:

$$
s_0\approx r_k.
$$

Then roll out a short local window:

$$
k\rightarrow k+H,
$$

perhaps initially \(H=8\sim32\) frames.

Reference time still advances normally:

$$
k,k+1,k+2,\dots
$$

but now the policy can directly learn the important transitions.

For example:

### Training batch A

Start at:

$$
k=20
$$

learn approach.

### Training batch B

Start at:

$$
k=35
$$

learn acquisition/contact.

### Training batch C

Start at:

$$
k=45
$$

learn grasp stabilization.

### Training batch D

Start at:

$$
k=55
$$

learn grasp-to-lift.

This eliminates the problem:

$$
\text{can't learn lift until it first discovers grasp from frame 0}.
$$

---

# 9. Then progressively increase horizon

This gives us a natural curriculum.

Initially:

$$
H=8.
$$

Then:

$$
H=16.
$$

Then:

$$
H=32.
$$

Eventually:

$$
H=T.
$$

At the final stage we run full episodes starting at frame 0.

This means the residual policy first learns **local physical corrections**, then learns to compose them into the complete trajectory.

That is much more compatible with OI-Cm, because OI-Cm itself is a one-step local model:

$$
(O_t,H_t,\Delta H_t)
\rightarrow
\Delta O_t.
$$

---

# 10. Early termination should replace phase freezing

Suppose at reference frame \(k\), the policy becomes hopelessly divergent.

Instead of keeping the reference frozen indefinitely, terminate that local rollout:

$$
D(s_t,r_t)>\epsilon
\Rightarrow
\text{reset}.
$$

This is much closer to what DExplore does.

The tolerance doesn't have to be arbitrary. We can establish it empirically from successful no-contact tracking.

For example, run the deterministic base without object interaction and obtain the distribution

$$
D_{\rm nominal}.
$$

Then define an envelope based on something like its upper quantile:

$$
\epsilon
=
Q_{0.95}(D_{\rm nominal})
+\text{margin}.
$$

That means:

> terminate only when the interaction has taken us well outside what normal tracking error explains.

This is cleaner than hand-writing “2 cm contact means move to the next phase.”

---

# 11. What should the residual actually observe?

I would drastically simplify this compared with feeding it the entire DExplore 1442-D observation.

For example:

$$
o_t^{RL}=
[
e^{hand}_t,\,
e^{obj}_t,\,
\dot e_t,\,
\Delta T^{ref}_{obj,t},
\,C_m(s_t)
].
$$

Where

$$
e^{hand}_t=q^{ref}_t-q_t
$$

or a more geometric link-space error,

$$
e^{obj}_t
=
(T^{ref}_{obj,t})^{-1}T_{obj,t},
$$

and

$$
\Delta T^{ref}_{obj,t}
=
(T^{ref}_{obj,t})^{-1}
T^{ref}_{obj,t+1}.
$$

Then Cm provides local interaction information.

The residual output remains small:

$$
\Delta a_t.
$$

This is conceptually much closer to our actual problem.

---

# 12. And what should OI-Cm do?

I would not initially use Cm to control phase.

I think that would be a mistake.

Its role should remain local:

$$
\text{candidate hand motion}
\rightarrow
\text{expected object response}.
$$

So:

$$
q^{ref}_{t+1}+\Delta q_t
$$

gives an FK hand sweep,

$$
\Delta H_t,
$$

and Cm predicts

$$
\hat{\Delta T}_{obj,t}.
$$

We know the desired effect from the reference:

$$
\Delta T^{ref}_{obj,t}.
$$

Then the policy can receive either the predicted error

$$
e^{Cm}_t
=
\hat{\Delta T}_{obj,t}
-
\Delta T^{ref}_{obj,t},
$$

or Cm can assist the critic / candidate-action scorer.

That is a much more direct use than “Cm decides whether phase advances.”

---

# 13. There is one experiment that decides whether this plan is viable

Before PPO, run:

$$
\boxed{\text{retargeted reference + deterministic PD + no object/contact}}
$$

or a ghost object.

If this cannot track the hand reference well, then our premise is wrong.

Measure:

$$
e_{\rm wrist}(t),
\qquad
e_{\rm finger}(t),
\qquad
e_{\rm tip}(t).
$$

If those errors stay small, then we've established:

$$
\boxed{\text{kinematic tracking does not need learning}}
$$

and the remaining degradation after enabling the physical object really is an **interaction problem**.

That's ideal for CmResidual.

If even ghost-object tracking fails badly, then I would change my recommendation: we need a low-level tracking layer. But even then I would first fix controller dynamics/feedforward rather than train a full DExplore-like PPO policy.

---

# 14. What I think is most likely to succeed

For **pure task performance**, my ordering would be:

1. Fine-tune DExplore on the target reference distribution.
2. Train a new DExplore-style policy.
3. Frozen DExplore zero-shot on DexYCB.

But that isn't the question our project is really asking.

For **our OI-Cm research objective**, my preferred route is:

1. **Retargeted reference → deterministic base PD target.**
2. Prove it can track accurately without interaction.
3. Keep reference time monotonically advancing—no main phase-freeze mechanism.
4. Train residual using short, phase-randomized/local reference windows.
5. Early terminate large deviations and resample.
6. Gradually increase rollout horizon.
7. Add OI-Cm as local effect information / candidate-action evaluator.
8. Finally evaluate whole trajectories from frame 0.

Schematically:

$$
\boxed{
r_t
\rightarrow
u_t^{base}
}
$$

$$
+
$$

$$
\boxed{
(s_t,r_t,C_m)
\rightarrow
\Delta u_t
}
$$

$$
=
u_t.
$$

Training starts locally:

$$
r_k\rightarrow r_{k+H},
$$

then eventually becomes:

$$
r_0\rightarrow r_T.
$$

That is the approach I currently think has the best balance of **probability of working, interpretability, and preserving a meaningful role for OI-Cm**.

The conceptual correction is therefore:

> **We don't need an adaptive phase controller to solve the problem DExplore solves. We need to train the residual so it can keep up with the reference clock, while using a curriculum that prevents early failures from blocking learning of later interaction transitions.**

DExplore solved that by training an enormous reference-conditioned policy over hundreds of GRAB sequences with PPO and reference-scoped termination. ([Proceedings of Machine Learning Research][1]) We can solve a smaller and cleaner version by keeping the nominal trajectory deterministic and applying the learning capacity only to the interaction residual.
