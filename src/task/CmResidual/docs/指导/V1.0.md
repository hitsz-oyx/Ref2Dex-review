Yes. **DexTrack is the closest open-source implementation to the base strategy we want**, and I would use it as the primary reference rather than implementing the tracker from first principles.

| ------------------------ | ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **DexTrack**             | **Very high**           | Dexterous hand-object tracking, kinematic references, PPO, residual positional targets         |

### DexTrack is the one I would start from

The official DexTrack repository is public and, importantly, it uses **Isaac Gym Preview 4 and `rl_games`**, which is very close to our existing environment. It supports a free-floating Allegro hand and a LEAP hand with Franka, and directly trains physics-based policies to track human-retargeted hand-object trajectories. ([GitHub][1])

Most importantly, it implements exactly the action formulation we've been discussing:

> **cumulated residual positional targets with kinematic bias**

as well as relative positional targets. The authors state that the cumulative-residual form is their original formulation. ([GitHub][1])

Conceptually that is

$$
a_t^{target}
=
a_t^{kinematic/ref}
+
\Delta a_t^{RL},
$$

or, for the cumulative version,

$$
a_t^{target}
=
a_t^{ref}
+
\sum_{i\le t}\Delta a_i.
$$

That's very close to what I think our first proper base controller should be.

They also provide both single-trajectory and multi-trajectory tracking environments and pretrained checkpoints/data. ([GitHub][1])

[DexTrack repository](https://github.com/Meowuu7/DexTrack?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### I would not copy DexTrack wholesale

There is one significant limitation. The released repository explicitly says that two important components of their full method—**homotopy optimization for specialist tracking and the IL+RL generalist iteration**—were not cleaned/released because they required substantial manual work. ([GitHub][1])

So what is open-sourced is enough for our **base tracker**, but not necessarily the complete DexTrack training recipe.

That's actually fine for us. We don't need to reproduce DexTrack's full paper.

I would port four concepts into `CmResidual`:

1. reference trajectory/phase handling;
2. cumulative or bounded residual positional targets around our coupled geometric reference;
3. proper hand + wrist + object tracking observations/rewards;
4. reference-state initialization plus perturbation/recovery training.

The fourth one is where I would write some of our own code.

## For RSI, I would look at MimicKit rather than old DeepMimic

The original DeepMimic code is still public, but its repository now explicitly says it is **deprecated** and recommends **MimicKit** instead. ([GitHub][2])

MimicKit is Xue Bin Peng's newer lightweight motion-imitation framework and already supports Isaac Gym, Isaac Lab and Newton, with DeepMimic-style PPO training over reference motion datasets. ([GitHub][3])

[MimicKit repository](https://github.com/xbpeng/MimicKit?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

I would use MimicKit mainly as a clean architectural reference for the **motion-tracking/reset side**, not transplant it into Ref2Dex.

For us, RSI needs one important modification anyway. We cannot simply reset

$$
q_t=q_t^{ref}.
$$

We need to restore the entire manipulation state coherently:

$$
S_\tau=
(q_\tau,\dot q_\tau,T^{wrist}_\tau,
T^{obj}_\tau,v^{obj}_\tau,\ldots).
$$

Then add controlled perturbations.

That is sufficiently specific to our data and object setup that I think implementing our own RSI inside `CmResidual` is preferable.

## ProtoMotions is worth knowing about, but I wouldn't migrate now

NVIDIA's ProtoMotions is now a fairly comprehensive open-source motion-control framework. It supports Isaac Gym, Isaac Lab, Newton, Genesis and MuJoCo, and provides a DeepMimic-style motion-imitation experiment directly. ([GitHub][4])

[ProtoMotions repository](https://github.com/NVlabs/ProtoMotions?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Architecturally it is cleaner and more modular than much of the older IsaacGym code.

But **moving Ref2Dex to ProtoMotions now would cost more than it gives us**. Our current repository is already based around IsaacGymEnvs/rl_games, and DexTrack uses essentially that same ecosystem.

I'd keep ProtoMotions in mind if we later migrate to Isaac Lab.

## ResMimic is the reference for our *later* architecture

ResMimic is also open source now. Its structure is especially relevant to the longer-term discussion we've been having: a general motion tracker acts as the base, and an additional learned residual specializes it for object manipulation. The released repository contains training/evaluation code for that residual policy. ([GitHub][5])

[ResMimic repository](https://github.com/amazon-far/ResMimic?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

It is humanoid whole-body manipulation rather than dexterous hand tracking, so I would **not** base our tracker on it.

But later, when we reach

$$
\pi_{\text{track}}
+
\delta a^{OI/RL},
$$

I would study its policy decomposition carefully.

# What I would actually do in our repository

I don't think we need a new external codebase.

The most efficient route is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{our coupled geometric data}
+
\text{DexTrack-style tracker}
+
\text{our IsaacGym environment}
}
$$

Keep `CmResidual` and `rl_games`.

Replace the current notion of `basePolicy` with a proper tracking experiment:

$$
\pi_{\text{track}}
(
s_t,
r_t,r_{t+1},r_{t+2},r_{t+H}
)
\rightarrow
\Delta a_t
$$

and execute

$$
a_t^{target}
=
a_t^{geo}
+
\Delta a_t.
$$

At reset:

$$
\tau\sim p(\tau),
$$

restore hand **and object** to reference state \(\tau\), and then optionally perturb them:

$$
S_0=S_\tau^{ref}+\epsilon.
$$

That gives us the genuine base policy we've been talking about.

I would therefore **use DexTrack as the implementation template, not as a dependency**. Its simulator/RL stack is close enough to ours that porting the relevant tracking logic should be much easier than importing another framework.

The nice consequence is that our scientific architecture then becomes easy to defend:

$$
\text{geometric retargeting}
\rightarrow
\underbrace{\text{DexTrack-style physics tracker}}_{\text{robust base}}
\rightarrow
\underbrace{\text{OI-Cm + residual RL}}_{\text{our contribution}}.
$$

I think that is currently the most sensible route.

Yes. At this point I would **stop treating geometric redirection as a trajectory that we simply replay**, and first turn it into a genuine **feedback tracking controller**. I would also include **RSI**, but for dexterous object manipulation it has to be implemented more carefully than standard DeepMimic RSI.

There is one nuance: I do **not** mean developing another sophisticated geometric optimizer. We already have the coupled geometric trajectory. I mean using it as the reference for training a physics-aware tracker:

$$
\boxed{
(s_t,\;r_{t:t+H})
\rightarrow
\pi_{\text{track}}
\rightarrow
a_t
}
$$

rather than

$$
\boxed{
r_t\rightarrow a_t^{geo}\rightarrow\text{PD controller}.
}
$$

That should become the base strategy.

## Why I now prefer this over direct replay

With replay,

$$
a_t=a_t^{geo}.
$$

If at time \(t\) the actual robot is already behind the reference,

$$
s_t\neq s_t^{ref},
$$

the controller still blindly goes to

$$
a_{t+1}^{geo}.
$$

The error therefore propagates.

A tracking policy instead receives both:

$$
s_t^{actual}
$$

and

$$
s_{t:t+H}^{ref},
$$

and learns

$$
\pi_{\text{track}}
(
s_t^{actual},
s_{t:t+H}^{ref}
)
\rightarrow
a_t.
$$

The policy can therefore learn:

> “I am 8 mm behind the reference and this finger hasn't established contact yet, so I should not simply execute frame \(t+1\).”

That feedback property is exactly what our current CmDecoder lacks.

This distinction is standard in physics-based motion tracking. DeepMimic uses RL to turn reference motion into a feedback policy capable of responding to perturbations rather than merely replaying it. ([arXiv][1]) DexTrack follows the same basic idea for dexterous manipulation: it starts from kinematically retargeted references and trains neural tracking controllers, including a cumulative residual positional-target action space with kinematic bias. ([GitHub][2])

So I think we should follow that path before testing OI-Cm.

# But we actually already have most of this structure

I wouldn't throw away `CmResidual`.

Conceptually, change it from:

$$
\text{geometric target}+\text{small task residual}
$$

to explicitly:

$$
\boxed{
a_t=
a_t^{geo}
+
\Delta a_t^{track}
}
$$

where PPO is trained primarily to be a **reference tracker**.

This is close to DexTrack's cumulative residual/kinematic-bias formulation. ([GitHub][2])

So our development path becomes:

$$
\text{coupled geometric trajectory}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{RL tracking policy}}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\text{robust base controller}.
$$

Only after that:

$$
\boxed{\text{tracking base}+\text{OI-Cm interaction knowledge}}
$$

becomes a meaningful experiment.

# I would definitely add RSI

RSI—Reference State Initialization—is particularly appropriate here.

Instead of every episode beginning at

$$
t=0,
$$

sample

$$
\tau\sim U(0,T)
$$

and initialize the simulator to the corresponding trajectory state.

DeepMimic introduced this precisely so the policy can learn all phases of a motion rather than first having to survive every preceding phase. It improves exploration and prevents later portions of complex motions from receiving almost no training signal. ([Cornell eCommons][3])

For our trajectories, that is even more important.

Without RSI, suppose contact starts around frame 70. Early PPO policies fail at frame 20–30.

Then the policy almost never experiences:

$$
\text{contact establishment},
$$

$$
\text{object motion},
$$

$$
\text{release/regrasp},
$$

etc.

The network therefore becomes very good at approach while the scientifically interesting contact region receives little data.

With RSI:

$$
t_0=10,\;40,\;75,\;110,\ldots
$$

all become starting points.

That fits our problem very well.

---

# But I would not implement naive RSI

This is important for manipulation.

For humanoid locomotion, RSI is roughly:

$$
q=q_\tau^{ref},
\qquad
\dot q=\dot q_\tau^{ref}.
$$

For our task, **hand and object must be initialized jointly**.

We need approximately:

$$
\boxed{
S_\tau=
(
q_\tau,\dot q_\tau,
T^{wrist}_\tau,
v^{wrist}_\tau,
T^o_\tau,
v^o_\tau
)
}
$$

and ideally any other dynamic state necessary for consistency.

If we initialize the robot from frame 80 but put the object at its frame-0 position, RSI becomes meaningless.

Similarly, if the retargeted hand geometry penetrates the object at the sampled frame, restoring that state can create an artificial contact impulse.

This is a known problem with RSI: invalid/interpenetrating reference states can generate extreme simulator reactions when used directly as reset states. ([ICLR 会议录][4])

So I would introduce a **valid-RSI mask**.

For each reference frame, offline check things such as:

$$
\text{penetration}<\epsilon,
$$

$$
\text{joint limits valid},
$$

$$
\text{object/hand state physically reasonable}.
$$

Only valid frames become RSI candidates.

---

# I would also mix RSI with normal initial-state training

I would not train on 100% random reference phases.

Deployment begins from the real initial condition.

So if every episode begins from a perfect mid-trajectory reference state, the policy may become excellent at recovery around a trajectory but surprisingly weak at entering the trajectory from the actual beginning.

Conceptually use a mixture:

$$
p(s_0)
=
\lambda p_{\text{RSI}}
+
(1-\lambda)p_{\text{start}}.
$$

I wouldn't treat one particular \(\lambda\) as sacred. Something around a balanced mixture is a reasonable first experiment, then ablate it.

Later we can make phase sampling adaptive.

If frame range 70–100 has poor success, increase its sampling probability:

$$
p(\tau)\propto f(\text{tracking difficulty}_\tau).
$$

Adaptive state initialization has also been used in motion-tracking work specifically to avoid spending most training on already-easy portions of reference motions. ([Liam Jing][5])

For our contact-rich task, I suspect adaptive RSI will eventually be more useful than uniform RSI.

---

# I would add perturbation on top of RSI

RSI alone teaches:

$$
s_\tau^{ref}
\rightarrow
\text{continue reference}.
$$

But we really need:

$$
s_\tau^{ref}+\epsilon
\rightarrow
\text{return toward useful trajectory}.
$$

So after restoring a valid reference state, perturb it moderately:

$$
q=q_\tau+\epsilon_q,
$$

$$
T_w=T_{w,\tau}\oplus\epsilon_w,
$$

$$
T_o=T_{o,\tau}\oplus\epsilon_o.
$$

Not huge perturbations initially.

A curriculum makes more sense:

$$
\epsilon_{\text{small}}
\rightarrow
\epsilon_{\text{medium}}
\rightarrow
\epsilon_{\text{larger}}.
$$

That directly trains the property we care about:

$$
\boxed{\text{local contraction/recovery}.}
$$

And notice how this differs from CmDecoder training.

Current supervised reconstruction mostly says:

$$
s^{ref}_t\rightarrow a_t^{ref}.
$$

Tracking RL with RSI/noise says:

$$
s_t^{perturbed}
\rightarrow
a_t^{corrective}.
$$

That's why I expect it to be much more robust.

---

# The observation should contain a future reference window

I would not only give

$$
r_t.
$$

Use something like:

$$
r_t,\quad
r_{t+1},\quad
r_{t+2},\quad
r_{t+4}
$$

or equivalent relative reference features.

Then the tracker knows the short-term intention.

AdaDexTrack is particularly informative here. Its tracker uses future-reference information, and its 2026 modulation framework explicitly modifies near-future references at offsets such as \(1,2,4,12\) to combat accumulated tracking error. ([CVF Open Access][6])

We don't need AdaDexTrack's full architecture.

But the principle is relevant:

$$
\boxed{\text{tracking should be state-feedback + short-horizon reference}}
$$

rather than current-frame replay.

# What should the tracking reward be?

Before adding Cm, I would make this intentionally conventional.

Something approximately like

$$
r_t=
w_qr_q+
w_wr_w+
w_or_o+
w_cr_c
-
w_a r_a.
$$

Where:

**Finger tracking**

$$
r_q=\exp(-k_q\|q_t-q_t^{ref}\|^2)
$$

**Wrist tracking**

$$
r_w=
\exp(
-k_p\|p_w-p_w^{ref}\|^2
-k_Rd(R_w,R_w^{ref})^2
)
$$

**Object tracking**

$$
r_o=
\exp(
-k_{op}\|p_o-p_o^{ref}\|^2
-k_{oR}d(R_o,R_o^{ref})^2
)
$$

**Contact/interaction**

Some modest term preserving intended hand-object proximity/contact.

**Regularization**

$$
r_a=\|\Delta a_t\|^2
$$

plus optionally action-rate/smoothness penalties.

The point is not to discover the perfect reward now.

The goal is for the tracker to learn:

$$
\boxed{
\text{follow the hand-object reference under actual physics}.
}
$$

DexTrack's entire premise is precisely physics-aware tracking from kinematic human-derived references, rather than trusting the kinematic trajectory itself. ([arXiv][7])

# One thing I would not do

I would **not include OI-Cm in this tracker yet**.

The base tracker should answer one clean question:

> Can the robot robustly physically track our coupled geometric hand-object references?

If it cannot, fix that first.

Otherwise when:

$$
\text{Tracker + Cm}
$$

fails, we won't know whether:

* Cm is bad,
* reference tracking is bad,
* PPO is bad,
* or their interaction is bad.

This base should therefore be intentionally boring.

That is useful science.

---

# Then OI-Cm becomes much more valuable

Once

$$
\pi_{\text{track}}
$$

is competent, we freeze it.

Now our real architecture can be:

$$
a_t^{base}
=
\pi_{\text{track}}(s_t,r_{t:t+H})
$$

and then an interaction-level policy adds:

$$
\delta a_t^{OI/RL}.
$$

So:

$$
\boxed{
a_t
=
a_t^{track}
+
\delta a_t^{interaction}.
}
$$

OI-Cm evaluates:

$$
(s_t,a_t)
\rightarrow
C_m
\rightarrow
\hat{\Delta O}.
$$

Now OI-Cm is no longer compensating for basic trajectory-tracking failure.

It can focus on exactly the difficult question we care about:

> Among actions that are all plausible ways of following the reference, which one creates the correct interaction?

That gives the project a much stronger decomposition.

---

## Eventually I would even allow the second policy to modify the reference

There is a limitation to residual action alone.

Suppose the physical hand has drifted substantially. The correct solution may not be:

$$
a_t^{track}+\delta a_t.
$$

The reference itself may need to slow down or move.

A later model could produce:

$$
\delta r_{t:t+H}
$$

as well as

$$
\delta a_t.
$$

Then:

$$
\tilde r=r+\delta r
$$

and

$$
a=\pi_{\text{track}}(s,\tilde r)+\delta a.
$$

That is broadly the direction AdaDexTrack takes: it explicitly modulates reference, object representation, and positional target to reduce fixed-reference drift. ([CVF Open Access][6])

And **that might ultimately be an excellent role for Cm**.

For example:

$$
C_m
\rightarrow
\text{“current interaction is lagging behind expected contact”}
$$

could cause

$$
\delta r
$$

to delay closure or adjust contact geometry rather than trying to fix everything through a huge instantaneous residual.

But that's a later stage.

---

# The concrete development order I would use now

I would change our earlier sequence slightly:

$$
\boxed{\textbf{Stage 0: Coupled geometric references}}
$$

Already mostly available.

Then:

$$
\boxed{\textbf{Stage 1: Genuine tracking RL}}
$$

Use:

* coupled geometric reference;
* current actual state;
* future reference window;
* residual positional targets;
* RSI;
* valid reference-state reset;
* modest state perturbations;
* tracking/object/contact rewards.

No Cm.

Then require this base tracker to demonstrate:

1. low hand/wrist tracking error;
2. substantially lower object drift than raw replay;
3. recovery from small perturbations;
4. good performance from both RSI and true frame-0 starts;
5. bounded/smooth residual actions.

Only then:

$$
\boxed{\textbf{Stage 2: Freeze tracker + add OI-Cm}}
$$

First critic/evaluation role.

Then:

$$
\boxed{\textbf{Stage 3: Cm-conditioned interaction residual}}
$$

and possibly later:

$$
\boxed{\textbf{Stage 4: Cm-based reference modulation / short-horizon model use}}.
$$

---

So my answer is **yes**: I now think a proper tracking base with RSI should come *before* serious OI-Cm/RL integration.

The reason is not merely that other motion-tracking systems use RSI. The deeper reason is that it establishes the missing hierarchy:

$$
\boxed{
\text{geometry tells us what motion to imitate}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{tracking RL learns how to execute it robustly under physics}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{OI-Cm/RL later learns how to alter that execution for better interaction}
}
$$

If we skip the middle layer, OI-Cm is forced to compensate for ordinary tracking errors, and we will have difficulty determining whether its interaction representation is genuinely useful.
